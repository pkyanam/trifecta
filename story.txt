The Lighthouse at the Edge of Tomorrow

Mira had kept the lighthouse for thirty-seven years, but tonight was the first time the light refused to turn.

She climbed the spiral stairs with a wrench in one hand and a thermos of black tea in the other, muttering at the gulls that had taken up residence in the third-floor window. The gears at the top of the tower were ancient — brass cogs older than her grandmother — and they had always turned, even through the great storm of '63 when the cliffs themselves had shifted six inches toward the sea.

But tonight the lamp sat still, staring out at a dark and waiting ocean.

When she reached the top, she found a boy sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the mechanism. He couldn't have been more than ten. His coat was made of something that shimmered like wet stone, and his eyes were the color of the horizon just before a squall.

"You're in my lighthouse," Mira said.

"I know," said the boy. "I stopped the gears. I needed to talk to you before the ships came."

Mira set down the thermos. She did not set down the wrench.

"What ships?"

The boy pointed out toward the black water. "The ones that have been lost. They've been circling for a hundred years, waiting for a light they could trust. Yours is the only one left that still turns by hand. The others all forgot how to listen."

Mira looked out at the sea. For a moment — just a moment — she thought she could see them: a hundred pale shapes drifting just beneath the surface of the dark, their lanterns dim, their sails like moth-wings.

"What do they need?" she asked.

"Someone to remember their names," the boy said. "That's all. A light is just a way of saying: I see you. I know you were here."

Mira knelt down beside the great brass mechanism. She set her hand on the cog where her grandmother's hand had rested, and her mother's after that. The metal was warm.

"Tell me the names," she said. "I'll turn the light for as long as it takes."

The boy smiled, and the gears began to move on their own.

Out on the water, one by one, a hundred small lanterns flickered back to life — and then, gently, like sleepers finally finding their way home, they began to drift toward the shore.

The End.
